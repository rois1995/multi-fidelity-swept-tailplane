\title{Multi-fidelity characterization of a positively swept tailplane with full-span control surface}

\author{Andrea Rausa\footnote{PhD, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology, Corresponding Author, andrea.rausa@polimi.it}, Giulio Gori\footnote{Associate Professor, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology}, Alex Zanotti \footnote{Associate Professor, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology}, Giuseppe Gibertini \footnote{Associate Professor, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology}, Alberto Guardone \footnote{Full Professor, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology}, and Franco Auteri \footnote{Full Professor, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology}}
\affil{Politecnico di Milano, Department of Aerospace Science and Technology, Milano, Italy, 20154}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
This work presents a multi-fidelity numerical--experimental investigation of a positively swept, zero-dihedral tailplane equipped with a full-span trailing-edge control surface.
Wind-tunnel measurements are compared with a nonlinear lifting-line model and three-dimensional Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes simulations over angles of attack from \(-26^\circ\) to \(26^\circ\) and control-surface deflections from \(0^\circ\) to \(34^\circ\).
The analysis is organized by aerodynamic regime to distinguish attached flow, control-surface separation, main-surface stall, and post-stall behavior.
Both numerical methods reproduce the main lift trends in attached and weakly nonlinear conditions, but the lifting-line model shows increasing discrepancies in pitching moment, control effectiveness, and separated-flow regimes.
The Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes simulations provide a more consistent description of the separated-flow topology, although they predict a stall onset sharper than observed experimentally.
Surface-flow visualizations show extensive separation at or shortly after the experimental maximum-lift incidence, consistent with a progressive development of separation, whereas the simulated flow remains largely attached up to the predicted maximum-lift state and then separates abruptly.
The resulting comparison defines the regimes in which each fidelity level remains informative and identifies the modeling assumptions that limit their accuracy for unconventional swept tailplanes with full-span control surfaces.
\end{abstract}







\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The horizontal tailplane contributes directly to aircraft stability, controllability, maneuvering capability, and trim. 
Its aerodynamics must therefore be predicted with sufficient accuracy across a broad range of angles of attack and considering all possible control-surface deflections.
This also includes predicting aerodynamics outside the nominal linear operating range. 
The challenge becomes extremely complex with advanced rear-end architectures, where structural, propulsive-integration, or packaging constraints may require an unconventional tailplane geometry.

In preliminary design, the aerodynamic response of a horizontal tail is often reduced to linearized derivatives with respect to incidence and control-surface deflection, such as the lift-curve slope \(C_{L,\alpha}=\partial C_L/\partial\alpha\) and lift control derivative \(C_{L,\delta}=\partial C_L/\partial\delta\), where \(C_L\) is the lift coefficient, \(\alpha\) the incidence, and \(\delta\) the control deflection.
This representation assumes that the lift and pitching-moment increments produced by the movable surface are nearly proportional to the imposed deflection around a reference operating condition.
Although this approximation is effective at small incidence and small deflection, its validity deteriorates when the pressure recovery over the control surface produces substantial boundary-layer deceleration or separation.

A full-span trailing-edge control surface can be particularly sensitive to this behavior because its deflection changes the effective camber and pressure distribution along the complete tailplane span. 
Control-surface separation may consequently alter the global lift and pitching moment before the main lifting surface reaches its conventional stall condition.

\citet{Dods1948} established the principal effects of sweep, aspect ratio, taper, and elevator deflection on lift, pitching moment, control effectiveness, and hinge moment in classical wind tunnel investigations of horizontal tails.
\citet{PurserSpearman1951} also demonstrated through experiments on swept-back, swept-forward, and yawed wings that planform geometry modifies the spanwise circulation distribution and the progression of stall.
These investigations provide a fundamental basis for interpreting swept-tail aerodynamics, but their configurations, airfoil sections, Reynolds numbers, and measurement techniques differ from those relevant to contemporary numerical validation studies.

Sweep affects both inviscid loading and boundary-layer development on a finite lifting surface. 
The component of the external flow parallel to the leading edge produces spanwise transport and can modify the local separation behavior at the root and tip. 
The resulting stall progression cannot, in general, be inferred directly from the two-dimensional characteristics of the sectional profile. 
When a control surface is deflected, the interaction between sweep-induced crossflow and the adverse pressure gradient downstream of the hinge line introduces a further source of three-dimensionality. 
The aerodynamic effectiveness of the control surface may therefore depend on incidence, spanwise position, and deflection amplitude rather than a single constant derivative.

Low-order aerodynamic methods remain indispensable during conceptual and preliminary design because they permit large geometrical and operating spaces to be investigated at limited computational cost. 
Classical lifting-line theory, developed by \citet{Prandtl1921}, represents a finite lifting surface through a spanwise distribution of bound circulation and the associated trailing-vortex system.
The original linear formulation is best suited to slender lifting surfaces operating in attached flow. 
\citet{SivellsNeely1947} extended its range of applicability by introducing nonlinear sectional aerodynamic data, allowing the local section response to approach maximum lift and stall.

Subsequent lifting-line and vortex-based formulations have incorporated sweep, taper, twist, nonplanar geometry, viscous sectional data, and unsteady effects. 
\citet{SugarGabor2018} and \citet{ReidHunsaker2021} developed formulations that determine the local effective incidence from the interaction between sectional aerodynamic characteristics and the induced velocity field.
\citet{GallayGhasemiLaurendeau2014} and \citet{GoitiaLlamas2019} proposed sweep-corrected and quasi-three-dimensional formulations to approximate the differences between two-dimensional sectional behavior and the response of a finite swept wing.

Despite these developments, the predictive capability of a nonlinear lifting-line model remains tied to the validity of its sectional closure. 
Locally applied airfoil polars cannot explicitly reproduce spanwise boundary-layer transport, junction flow, tip-vortex interaction, or changes in the topology of strongly separated flow. 
Moreover, using two-dimensional post-stall characteristics may introduce an incorrect separation onset or inaccurately redistribute loading along the span. 
A model can therefore reproduce an integrated lift coefficient while retaining significant errors in the local loading or pitching moment. 
Establishing the operating range over which the sectional approximation remains adequate is therefore an important part of validating such methods.

Reynolds-averaged Navier--Stokes methods provide higher physical resolution while retaining a computational cost compatible with systematic aerodynamic analysis. 
RANS simulations resolve the three-dimensional pressure and mean boundary-layer fields and allow detailed examination of surface loading and separated-flow topology. 
Their reliability is generally strongest in attached and mildly separated flow.
Near stall, the predicted onset and extent of separation may depend strongly on turbulence closure, laminar-to-turbulent transition modeling, grid resolution, boundary conditions, and the assumption that the flow can be represented by a steady mean solution.

A meaningful comparison of RANS calculations with wind tunnel measurements must also distinguish model-form error from differences between the numerical and experimental configurations. 
The computational model should reproduce the experimental geometry, Reynolds and Mach numbers, control setting, boundary-layer state, and relevant tunnel boundaries as closely as possible. 
Numerical verification and experimental uncertainty must be considered alongside the coefficient differences~\citep{ClarkEtAl2020}. 
Wind tunnel data are therefore treated in the present study as an uncertainty-characterized experimental validation reference rather than as an error-free ground truth.

The complementary capabilities of experimental measurements, high-fidelity simulations, and low-order aerodynamic models motivate a multi-fidelity approach. 
In such a framework, the inexpensive model provides broad parameter-space coverage, the higher-fidelity model provides detailed flow information, and the experiment establishes the physical reference against which discrepancies between models can be assessed~\citep{PeherstorferWillcoxGunzburger2018}. 
The objective is not simply to determine which model best fits the experiment on average, but to identify the aerodynamic regimes in which each modeling assumption remains valid.

The Clean Sky 2 MONNALISA project was established to develop and validate this type of methodology for unconventional lifting surfaces with controls~\cite{auteri2022monnalisa}. 
The project generated an experimental and numerical database covering variations in sweep, aspect ratio, taper ratio, dihedral, angle of attack, and full-span control-surface deflection. 
Initial work addressed numerical comparisons of unconventional tail configurations~\citep{RausaEtAl2022}, experimental characterization of the boundary-layer transition state~\citep{ZanottiEtAl2022}, and Bayesian calibration of the low-order aerodynamic model~\citep{GoriEtAl2023}.

The structure, methodologies, and contents of the complete MONNALISA database were subsequently documented by~\citet{rausa2026monnalisa}. 
That study presented the full geometrical parameterization, computational and experimental procedures, database organization, and an initial comparison of the available fidelity levels. 
The comparison indicated general coherence among the experimental, lifting-line, and RANS data in the approximately linear regime, with increasing discrepancies at large incidence and control deflection. 
The database paper primarily documented available resources and did not provide a complete, configuration-specific analysis of control effectiveness, separation development, local loading, or numerical model validity.

The present study addresses this gap for the positively swept, zero-dihedral reference tailplane.
The configuration is investigated across the full available incidence--deflection space using wind tunnel measurements, a nonlinear lifting-line model, and three-dimensional RANS simulations.
Particular attention is devoted to how lift and pitching-moment effectiveness vary with incidence, the onset and development of control-surface separation, the transition to main-surface stall, and the physical origins of disagreement among the fidelity levels.

The principal objectives are to quantify the agreement among the three approaches in the attached, separated, and post-stall regimes; determine when control effectiveness can no longer be represented by constant derivatives; relate changes in the global aerodynamic coefficients to the observed surface-flow topology; and provide a regime-dependent assessment of the range in which each numerical method remains useful.
The analysis is organized by aerodynamic mechanism rather than by data source, so that experimental, lifting-line, and RANS results are compared directly within each flow regime.
This structure avoids reducing the comparison to a single global error measure and instead identifies the modeling assumptions that limit each fidelity level.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:configuration} defines the selected geometry, operating conditions, and aerodynamic quantities.
Sec.~\ref{sec:methodology} summarizes the experimental and numerical methods, with detailed derivations and database procedures referred to the preceding MONNALISA publication.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the multi-fidelity comparison, the analysis of the governing flow mechanisms, and the regime-dependent assessment of the numerical methods.

\subsection{Tailplane geometry}
\label{sec:geometry}

The configuration considered is the positively swept reference tailplane of the MONNALISA database~\citep{rausa2026monnalisa} with zero dihedral.
Namely, a low-aspect-ratio trapezoidal wing with a quarter-chord sweep of $30^\circ$, an aspect ratio of $5$, and a taper ratio of $0.4$. 
In this convention, positive sweep means the tip is downstream of the root. 
The semispan is $b_s=1.6~\mathrm{m}$, the semispan reference area is $S_{\mathrm{ref}}=1.024~\mathrm{m^2}$, and the mean aerodynamic chord is $\bar{c}=0.679184~\mathrm{m}$.

The tailplane uses a single symmetric airfoil, scaled to the local chord along the full span.
Its maximum thickness is $9\%$ of the local chord. 
A control surface is located at the trailing edge and extends over the full semispan.
The control surface rotates about a hinge located at $70\%$ of the local chord. 
The control surface dimension is therefore $30\%$ of the local tailplane chord. 
The discrete control settings considered in the aerodynamic campaign are \(\delta \in \{0^\circ,10^\circ,20^\circ,30^\circ,34^\circ\}\).

\section{Experimental and Numerical Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The experimental and numerical data analyzed in this work are part of the MONNALISA database. Detailed descriptions of the complete test campaign, numerical formulations, grid-generation procedure, wind tunnel corrections, and database organization are provided by \citet{rausa2026monnalisa}. This section summarizes the information needed to interpret the configuration-specific comparison and highlights the assumptions that affect the validation results.

\subsection{Wind Tunnel Experiments}
\label{sec:wind_tunnel}

The experiments were performed in the Low Turbulence Test Section of the large closed-circuit wind tunnel at Politecnico di Milano. The effective test section was approximately $4~\mathrm{m}$ wide, $3.49~\mathrm{m}$ high, and $6~\mathrm{m}$ long. A dummy floor was installed to enclose the model support, the aerodynamic balance, and the control-surface actuation mechanism. The tailplane was mounted as a vertical semispan model, and the angle of attack was varied by rotating the base of the supporting frame.

The installation is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:wind_tunnel_installation}.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[angle=-90,width=0.48\linewidth]{WindTunnelInstallation.jpg}
    \caption{Reference semispan tailplane installed vertically above the wind tunnel dummy floor.}
    \label{fig:wind_tunnel_installation}
\end{figure}

The maximum test-section velocity is approximately $55~\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}$, and the freestream turbulence intensity is below $0.1\%$. Measurements of the total-pressure distribution showed variations within approximately $0.5\%$ of the dynamic pressure over most of the test section and below approximately $1\%$ elsewhere outside the wall boundary layers. The chamber is slightly divergent to compensate for boundary-layer growth, leaving a small residual streamwise static-pressure gradient.

The aerodynamic forces and moments were measured using an RUAG 767 strain-gauge balance installed beneath the dummy floor. The balance measured the loads acting on the complete semispan model. The measured forces and moments were nondimensionalized using the freestream dynamic pressure and the reference quantities in Eq.~\ref{eq:aero_coefficients}. The measured loads were transformed from the balance reference system to the wind-axis system before comparison with the numerical results.

The pitching moment was evaluated about a spanwise axis at the streamwise aerodynamic-center coordinate, measured from the root leading edge. Its definition does not require a spanwise location on the mean aerodynamic chord: translating a reference point along the moment axis leaves the pitching moment unchanged. The position of this point was determined from the approximately linear variation of force and moment with incidence for the undeflected configuration and was subsequently kept fixed for all values of $\delta$. This procedure allows consistent comparison of the control-induced change in pitching moment between the experimental and numerical datasets.

The measured data were corrected for horizontal buoyancy, solid blockage, wake blockage, and the modification of the effective angle of attack produced by the test-section boundaries. The complete correction procedure is reported by \citet{rausa2026monnalisa} and follows standard low-speed wind tunnel practice \citep{barlow1999low}. The present analysis uses the corrected aerodynamic coefficients and corrected angle of attack.

The experimental error bars are the uncertainties supplied with the database, propagated through the complete measurement and data-reduction chain, including the balance and the transformation to the wind-axis system~\citep{rausa2026monnalisa}. The pitching moment about the aerodynamic center also retains the propagated uncertainty of the fitted aerodynamic-center position.

Surface-flow visualizations are used to compare the experimental separation patterns with the RANS skin-friction lines.

A relevant difference between the experimental and numerical configurations occurs at the model root. In the wind tunnel, the root region is immersed in the boundary layer developing over the dummy floor. In the RANS calculations, the corresponding plane is represented using a symmetry boundary condition. This difference is expected to have a limited effect on the attached flow but can influence the inboard movement and spanwise extent of separated structures. This difference is therefore considered explicitly when interpreting the post-stall results.


\subsection{Nonlinear lifting-line model}
\label{sec:lifting_line}

The low-order predictions were obtained using the nonlinear lifting-line model implemented in DUST \citep{TUGNOLI2021106804}. A one-dimensional spanwise distribution of bound circulation represents the lifting surface. The trailing vortex system induces a velocity field that modifies the local effective incidence at each lifting-line element.

For a generic spanwise position, the effective incidence may be expressed as
%
\begin{equation}
\alpha_{\mathrm{eff}}(\eta)
=
\alpha_{\mathrm{geom}}(\eta)
-
\alpha_{\mathrm{ind}}(\eta),
\label{eq:effective_incidence}
\end{equation}
%
where $\alpha_{\mathrm{ind}}$ results from the velocity induced by the complete circulation and wake system. The local lift, drag, and pitching-moment coefficients are interpolated from sectional polars as functions of effective incidence, Reynolds number, Mach number, and control deflection.

The tabulated characteristics are obtained from C81 lookup tables generated by two-dimensional RANS calculations of the sectional profile. Separate sectional geometries and polars are used for the different control-surface deflections. Control deflection is therefore represented through the aerodynamic response of the deflected profile rather than through a prescribed linear control-effectiveness factor.
The nonlinear $\Gamma$ method determines the circulation distribution by enforcing consistency between the sectional aerodynamic data and the Kutta--Joukowski relation. Iterations continue until the circulation distribution, induced incidence, and local sectional loads are mutually consistent.

Because the tailplane is swept, the two-dimensional sectional characteristics are modified using the quasi-three-dimensional correction proposed by \citet{GoitiaLlamas2019}. The correction combines the conventional transformation of the sectional data with a span-dependent modification of the effective sweep:
%
\begin{equation}
\lambda_{\mathrm{eff}}(\eta)
=
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}(\eta)
\kappa(\eta),
\label{eq:effective_sweep}
\end{equation}
%
where $\kappa(\eta)$ includes separate empirical contributions in the root and tip regions. The detailed functional form and its implementation in the MONNALISA model are documented by \citet{rausa2026monnalisa} and are not repeated here.

The lifting-line solution provides global aerodynamic coefficients and spanwise distributions of circulation and sectional loads at a small computational cost. It does not resolve the boundary-layer field or the three-dimensional topology of separated flow. Its ability to reproduce control-surface stall and main-surface separation depends on the sectional polars and on how well the sweep correction represents the actual spanwise redistribution of loading. These limitations are central to the comparison developed in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes model}
\label{sec:rans}

The higher-fidelity numerical predictions were obtained using the SU2 open-source computational framework \citep{palacios2013stanford}. The steady compressible RANS equations were discretized using a vertex-centered finite-volume method on an unstructured median-dual grid.

Convective fluxes were evaluated using the Roe approximate Riemann solver~\citep{Roe1981}. Second-order spatial accuracy was obtained using MUSCL reconstruction~\citep{van1979towards}, with a Venkatakrishnan limiter~\citep{venkatakrishnan1993accuracy} to reduce spurious oscillations in regions with strong solution gradients. An implicit pseudo-time-integration procedure advanced the equations toward a steady solution~\citep{palacios2013stanford}.

Turbulence closure was provided by the Spalart--Allmaras one-equation model \citep{spalart1992sa}. Laminar-to-turbulent transition was represented using the algebraic Bas--Cakmakcioglu formulation \citep{cakmakcioglu2020bcm}. A transition-sensitive model is relevant because infrared measurements indicate that the boundary-layer state varies with incidence and spanwise position \citep{ZanottiEtAl2022}.

The tailplane surface was modeled as an adiabatic no-slip wall. Freestream conditions were imposed on the external far-field boundaries, and the model root was represented as a symmetry plane. The resulting root-region difference is discussed in Sec.~\ref{sec:wind_tunnel}.

The computational meshes were generated using an automated procedure based on a parametric CAD representation and Pointwise. Anisotropic surface elements were used near the leading edge and control-surface junction, while a hex-dominant, octree-based volume mesh was used away from the wall. Additional refinement was introduced near the tail tip and along an incidence-dependent wake region. Near-wall layers were constructed to resolve the boundary layer consistently with the turbulence and transition models.

A four-level mesh study was previously performed for representative attached and separated conditions. For the zero-dihedral geometry, the nominal meshes contained approximately $10$--$13$ million grid points, depending on incidence, while the finest meshes contained up to approximately $65$ million points. Changes in the integrated lift and drag coefficients relative to the finest meshes remained below approximately $4\%$, although strictly monotonic convergence was not obtained in every case. Since this work uses the same numerical solutions, the existing grid study is retained \citep{rausa2026monnalisa}.

Convergence was monitored using the mean variation of the lift and drag coefficients over a prescribed iteration window:

%
\begin{equation}
\Delta C_i
=
\frac{1}{N_w}
\sum_{j=N-N_w+1}^{N}
\left|
C_{i,j}-C_{i,j-1}
\right|,
\qquad
i\in\{L,D\}.
\label{eq:load_convergence}
\end{equation}
%
A calculation was classified as converged when both load variations were below $3\times10^{-6}$. 
In the separated and post-stall regimes, a small residual or mean load increment does not, by itself, demonstrate that a steady RANS solution is physically representative of an experimentally unsteady flow.
The post-stall RANS results are therefore interpreted primarily in terms of mean load levels and a qualitatively separated-flow topology.

\subsection{Basis of the multi-fidelity comparison}
\label{sec:comparison_basis}

The experimental, lifting-line, and RANS data are compared at nominally identical values of angle of attack, control deflection, Mach number, and Reynolds number.
All three datasets use the same reference area, mean aerodynamic chord, and sign conventions.
For each dataset, the pitching moment is evaluated about the aerodynamic center estimated from the undeflected configuration in the approximately linear incidence range.
The same moment origin is then retained within each dataset for all control-surface deflections.

The comparison uses the corrected experimental angle of attack, the same sign convention for control-surface deflection and pitching moment, and the realized maximum control-surface setting of \(\delta=34^\circ\) in the wind tunnel.
The numerical calculations were performed at the nominal maximum setting, \(\delta=35^\circ\); the corresponding numerical curves are labeled \(\delta=34^\circ\) for brevity, and the last numerical deflection interval is computed with its actual \(5^\circ\) width.
Missing or nonstationary numerical conditions are excluded from quantitative comparisons.
No interpolation is applied across abrupt stall transitions. Experimental uncertainty, numerical discretization error, and model-form discrepancy are treated separately.
The negative-incidence numerical branches were reconstructed using the symmetry of the airfoil and opposite control deflections; they are not independent simulations.


\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

The results are organized by increasing aerodynamic complexity, starting from the undeflected configuration and then introducing the effects of control-surface deflection.
The undeflected case first establishes the baseline behavior of the positively swept tailplane and assesses the numerical models' ability to reproduce the attached-flow, stall, and post-stall regimes.
The subsequent subsections examine the effect of control deflection on global loads, the corresponding control effectiveness, and the flow mechanisms responsible for discrepancies among fidelity levels.

\subsection{Baseline aerodynamics of the undeflected tailplane}
\label{sec:baseline_results}


The undeflected configuration, $\delta=0^\circ$, provides the reference aerodynamic behavior of the positively swept tailplane in the absence of control-surface-induced separation.
This case is therefore used to assess the intrinsic effect of sweep on the aerodynamic coefficients and to identify the baseline limitations of the lifting-line and RANS models before considering nonzero control deflections.

Starting from the lift coefficient, two main regimes can be identified: an approximately linear attached-flow regime and a nonlinear stall/post-stall regime.
The linear regime extends approximately over $-10^\circ \leq \alpha \leq 10^\circ$, where both numerical approaches are in good agreement with the experimental data.
For $\lvert \alpha \rvert > 10^\circ$, all curves exhibit a visible change in trend, although the transition from attached to separated flow differs between the experiments and the numerical predictions.

At this incidence, the lifting-line prediction closely matches the wind-tunnel value of $C_{L,S}$, whereas the RANS solution overpredicts it.
This difference is consistent with the sharper stall transition predicted by the RANS simulations, in which the lift curve departs abruptly from the attached-flow trend.

The maximum lift coefficient provides a complementary indication of the post-stall behavior.
The experimental data reach $C_{L,\max}=0.8219$ at $\alpha=20^\circ$, showing that the lift continues to increase after the initial stall onset.
The lifting-line model predicts a comparable maximum lift, although it occurs later, at $\alpha=24^\circ$.
The RANS simulations instead reach their maximum value already at $\alpha=12^\circ$, because the initial slope break coincides with the peak of the computed lift curve.
Despite this difference in the predicted incidence of maximum lift, the RANS results reproduce the qualitative post-stall behavior of the positively swept configuration more consistently, namely, retaining a significant lift level after stall. This is consistent with the persistence of a coherent suction-side vortical structure~\citep{harper1964sweptstall}.
The lifting-line method provides reasonable integrated lift levels but exhibits a less regular post-stall evolution, reflecting the difficulty of obtaining a robust sectional-data-based solution when large portions of the span operate beyond stall.

The drag coefficient confirms the overall consistency of the numerical predictions with the experimental data.
The attached-flow region is clearly identifiable also from the drag curve and is again associated with $\lvert\alpha\rvert \leq 10^\circ$.
The RANS simulations provide the closest agreement over the full incidence range, particularly in the attached-flow regime.
However, the RANS curve appears to delay the departure from the attached-flow trend up to approximately $\alpha=12^\circ$, suggesting that the onset of separation is predicted slightly later than in the experiment.
Conversely, the lifting-line results show a marked change in the drag trend already around $\alpha=8^\circ$, indicating an earlier onset of separation in the underlying sectional response.
As the absolute incidence increases, the agreement with the experimental drag remains acceptable, although the lower-fidelity prediction progressively departs from the measurements and exhibits an oscillatory behavior consistent with that observed in the lift curve.

For nonzero deflections, however, the same reference point is retained within each dataset to compare control-induced load redistribution consistently.
However, as the incidence increases and the flow becomes nonlinear, the numerical pitching-moment curves depart from the experimental ones.

The main differences arise at the transition from attached flow to stall, where the experiments exhibit a more progressive evolution than the numerical predictions.

This baseline assessment provides the reference against which the following subsection examines the additional nonlinearities introduced by control-surface deflection.



\subsection{Effect of full-span control-surface deflection}
\label{sec:deflection_results}

After establishing the baseline aerodynamic behavior of the undeflected tailplane, this subsection examines the effect of full-span control-surface deflection.
The objective is to determine how deflection modifies global loads, the onset of nonlinear behavior, and agreement across fidelity levels.
Compared with the undeflected configuration, a positive trailing-edge deflection increases the section's effective camber and introduces a stronger adverse pressure gradient over the rear portion of the airfoil.
As a result, local separation over the control surface may occur before main-surface stall.
The comparison is first performed in terms of the global aerodynamic coefficients and is then used to identify the incidence and deflection ranges where the numerical predictions depart from the wind tunnel measurements.

All available deflections are included in the quantitative analysis.
However, to preserve the readability of the coefficient plots, the main figures show only selected representative deflections.
The complete set of curves is reported in Appendix~\ref{app:additional_coefficients}.
The main text focuses on the deflections that best illustrate the transition from attached-flow behavior to control-surface separation and main-surface stall.

Figure~\ref{fig:Coefficients:ExpvsNum:Delta0_10_20_34} compares the wind tunnel, RANS, and DUST lifting-line aerodynamic coefficients for $\delta\in\{0^\circ,10^\circ,20^\circ,34^\circ\}$.
As the control surface is deflected, the symmetry of the undeflected configuration is lost.
Consequently, the aerodynamic-coefficient curves at positive and negative incidences differ markedly.

Starting from the lift coefficient, the primary effect of positive control-surface deflection is a vertical shift of the curves.
This trend is consistent with the increase in effective camber produced by a downward deflection of the trailing-edge control surface~\cite{abbott1959theory}.
As a consequence, the maximum lift coefficient generally increases, while the corresponding incidence shifts toward lower values.
Both numerical approaches reproduce this behavior, although the fidelity differences become more pronounced as deflection increases.

For $\delta=10^\circ$, the numerical predictions remain in good agreement with the experimental data over most of the attached-flow range.
The RANS and lifting-line results are similar up to approximately $\alpha=8^\circ$.
At larger incidences, the RANS solution predicts an abrupt loss of lift, consistent with the sharp stall behavior already observed for the undeflected configuration.
The lifting-line prediction instead continues to increase up to approximately $\alpha=10^\circ$ and then approaches a plateau.
For this moderate deflection, the lifting-line model predicts a smoother post-stall evolution that aligns more closely with the experimental trend, although it overpredicts lift in part of the nonlinear range.
Conversely, the RANS solution underpredicts wind-tunnel lift after the abrupt slope change.

As deflection increases, the lifting-line results deviate more substantially from the experimental data.
This behavior is expected because the flow complexity associated with large control-surface deflections is not fully represented by the two-dimensional sectional data used in the C81 tables.
In particular, localized separation over the deflected surface, spanwise load redistribution, and three-dimensional separated-flow effects become increasingly important.
These effects are more directly represented in the RANS simulations, although discrepancies remain, especially in the pre-stall nonlinear range.

The lift curves exhibit a localized slope change before main-surface stall, most clearly for \(\delta\geq20^\circ\).
The load decomposition below associates this event with control-surface separation while the fixed surface remains mostly attached, allowing global lift to recover with increasing incidence.
The present comparison focuses on the differences in the predicted onset and the resulting control effectiveness, rather than a detailed reconstruction of the pre-stall mechanism.

The drag coefficient exhibits the expected upward shift as the control-surface deflection increases.
Moreover, the incidence corresponding to the minimum drag coefficient, $\alpha_{C_{D,\min}}$, moves toward lower values, consistent with the camber increase induced by the deflected trailing edge.
Another feature in the complete deflection set is that the wind-tunnel value of $C_{D,\min}$ changes only moderately up to $\delta=20^\circ$, whereas it increases more pronouncedly for the largest deflections.
This behavior suggests that, beyond a sufficiently large deflection, the control surface is already separated over a significant range of incidence, including conditions close to minimum drag.

The local $C_D$ contribution further clarifies the origin of the drag difference between the two deflections.
For $\delta=34^\circ$, the entire control surface contributes positively to drag.
The fixed surface, by contrast, shows regions of both positive and negative local drag contributions, primarily from the combined effects of the pressure distribution and local surface orientation.

This contribution partly counterbalances the positive drag generated over the rest of the deflected surface, reducing integrated control-surface drag and, therefore, the total drag coefficient.



Returning to the global coefficients, Fig.~\ref{fig:Coefficients:ExpvsNum:Delta0_10_20_34} shows that the pitching-moment coefficient is also strongly affected by the redistribution of aerodynamic loading caused by control-surface deflection.
The effect of deflection is not limited to a nearly uniform shift of $C_{M_y}$.
Instead, the slope and nonlinear behavior of the pitching-moment curve are modified as separation develops over the deflected surface and, at larger incidences, over the fixed portion of the tailplane.
Since the aerodynamic center used for the moment reduction is computed from the undeflected case, only the $\delta=0^\circ$ curve exhibits an approximately constant moment in the low-incidence attached-flow range.
For nonzero deflections, the pitching moment varies across the entire incidence sweep because the load distribution changes both in magnitude and in position relative to the reference point.

In the pre-stall range, the wind tunnel curves show a variation of the $C_{M_y}$--$\alpha$ slope around $\alpha=0^\circ$.
This behavior is consistent with the progressive redistribution of lift between the fixed and control surfaces.
As the fixed surface generates increasing positive lift, the inboard attached regions contribute to the pitching moment according to their position relative to the aerodynamic-center axis.
These inboard regions remain attached up to higher incidences and therefore continue to affect $C_{M_y}$ well into the nonlinear regime.
At the same time, the deflected control surface adds a contribution whose sign depends on its moment arm relative to the aerodynamic center.
When separation develops over the control surface, the corresponding loss of local lift produces an abrupt change in the pitching-moment coefficient.
This mechanism is visible, for example, around $\alpha\simeq0^\circ$ for $\delta=20^\circ$.

The numerical approaches differ substantially in their ability to reproduce the pitching-moment behavior.
The lifting-line model shows significant discrepancies for nonzero deflections, indicating that predicting integrated lift alone is insufficient to recover the correct chordwise and spanwise distributions of the aerodynamic load.
This limitation is particularly important for $C_{M_y}$ because the moment coefficient depends directly on the load position relative to the aerodynamic center.
The RANS simulations more closely represent the wind tunnel curve shapes, including the rise of $C_{M_y}$ around $\alpha=0^\circ$.
However, the RANS results replace the smoother post-stall evolution observed experimentally with sharper slope changes, consistent with the more abrupt separation behavior already identified in the lift and drag curves.



\subsection{Lift and pitching-moment control effectiveness}
\label{sec:effectiveness_results}


The previous subsection showed that control-surface deflection modifies the global aerodynamic coefficients through increased effective camber, control-surface separation, and load redistribution over the fixed surface.
A more compact assessment of these effects comes from examining the effectiveness of lift and pitching-moment control.
The objective is to quantify the incremental response of the deflected surface and determine how this response varies with incidence and deflection amplitude.
The derivatives are reported per radian, and no smoothing is applied to the finite-difference curves.

In this study, the interval control effectiveness is the finite-difference approximation of the control derivative at fixed incidence. For \(C_Q\), with \(Q\in\{L,M_y\}\), it is computed as
\begin{equation}
C_{Q,\delta}^{[\delta_i,\delta_j]}(\alpha)
=
\frac{
C_Q(\alpha,\delta_j)-C_Q(\alpha,\delta_i)
}{
\delta_j-\delta_i
},
\label{eq:control_effectiveness_interval}
\end{equation}
where \(\delta\) is expressed in radians.
The effectiveness is evaluated over the intervals \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\), \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\), \([20^\circ,30^\circ]\), and \([30^\circ,34^\circ]\).
These are secant slopes over finite deflection intervals, rather than exact local derivatives. The first interval represents the low-deflection control effectiveness, whereas the subsequent intervals quantify the finite-deflection response as the aerodynamic behavior becomes progressively nonlinear.

Each method's pitching moment is referred to its own aerodynamic center.
    Grey bands mark the averaging ranges of Table~\ref{tab:control_effectiveness}.
    In the last column, the numerical interval is \([30^\circ,35^\circ]\).

\begin{figure}
    \centering

    \caption{Lift and pitching-moment control effectiveness for successive deflection intervals; grey bands indicate the incidence averaging ranges.}
    \label{fig:AeroCoeffs:control_effectiveness}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:AeroCoeffs:control_effectiveness} reports \(C_{L,\delta}\) and \(C_{M_y,\delta}\) as functions of incidence for the selected deflection intervals.
Each column corresponds to a single deflection interval, and each panel compares the wind tunnel, RANS, and lifting-line results.
This layout allows direct assessment of method-to-method agreement for each deflection range, while the left-to-right variation highlights changes in control effectiveness as deflection amplitude increases.
Using the full incidence range also shows the different behavior of the negative- and positive-incidence branches.
At negative incidence, the deflected control surface remains attached over a wider range of conditions, and the lift control effectiveness is generally higher.
At positive incidence, the stronger adverse pressure gradient over the deflected surface promotes earlier separation and leads to a more rapid degradation of control effectiveness.

For the low-deflection interval \(\delta\in[0^\circ,10^\circ]\), the \(C_{L,\delta}\) curve exhibits an approximately constant value over the negative and low-incidence range.
This behavior indicates that when the flow remains attached to both the fixed and control surfaces, the lift response to deflection is nearly linear.
The wind tunnel results show an initial reduction in control effectiveness at positive incidences, consistent with the change in the \(C_L\)--\(\alpha\) slope visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:Coefficients:ExpvsNum:Delta0_10_20_34}.
Both numerical approaches reproduce the attached-flow level with reasonable accuracy, although they slightly overpredict the lift control derivative.
However, the numerical results do not capture the experimentally observed initial loss of effectiveness at small positive incidence.
As incidence increases further on both branches and separation develops over larger portions of the surface, the wind tunnel curve shows a progressive reduction in lift effectiveness.
The RANS simulations predict a more abrupt degradation, consistent with the sharper transition between attached and separated states already observed in the global lift curves.
The lifting-line prediction also captures the overall reduction of effectiveness, but its behavior becomes less regular as the flow enters the nonlinear regime.

For the interval \(\delta\in[10^\circ,20^\circ]\), \(C_{L,\delta}\) becomes more strongly dependent on incidence.
On the positive-incidence branch, the wind tunnel curve decreases at low incidence, partially recovers, and then decreases again as the flow approaches stall and post-stall conditions.
This trend is consistent with the interaction between control-surface separation and the still-attached flow over the fixed surface.
The numerical results reproduce the qualitative shape of the curve, although they overestimate the lift effectiveness at low positive incidence.
This is consistent with the numerically predicted delayed onset of control-surface separation in the global-coefficient analysis.
At higher positive incidences, the agreement with the wind tunnel data improves because the flow over larger portions of the surface has entered the separated regime.
On the negative-incidence branch, the larger values of \(C_{L,\delta}\) confirm that the control surface remains more effective when the local flow over the deflected surface is less prone to separation.

For the interval \(\delta\in[20^\circ,30^\circ]\), the wind tunnel lift effectiveness falls to approximately zero for \(-6^\circ\leq\alpha\leq-2^\circ\), and then recovers to approximately \(0.9\) at \(\alpha=4^\circ\).
In this incidence range, increasing the deflection from \(20^\circ\) to \(30^\circ\) produces no additional lift.
This indicates that the incremental lift generated as the deflection increases from \(20^\circ\) to \(30^\circ\) is no longer governed by a simple attached-flow response.
Instead, it reflects the incidence-dependent evolution of control-surface separation and the redistribution of the load over the fixed surface.
At larger positive incidences, the derivative tends to level off, consistent with the lift-retaining behavior of positively swept lifting surfaces in the post-stall regime.
The RANS simulations agree with the wind tunnel data at higher incidences, especially after stall onset.
Around \(\alpha=-2^\circ\), however, RANS predicts a negative lift effectiveness of approximately \(-0.5\), that is, a loss of lift with increasing deflection, which is not observed experimentally.
The model therefore does not predict the onset of control-surface separation over the correct incidence range.
The lifting-line model shows larger deviations when the aerodynamic surface is largely separated, confirming the reduced robustness of the sectional-data-based approach in this regime.

For the interval \(\delta\in[30^\circ,34^\circ]\), the lift effectiveness is lower over most of the incidence range.
This behavior is consistent with the fact that, at these large deflections, the control surface is already separated over a substantial portion of the operating envelope.
Because this derivative is computed over a narrow \(4^\circ\) deflection interval, the corresponding curves are more sensitive to local variations and should be interpreted more cautiously than the wider \(10^\circ\) intervals.
The near-zero plateau observed in the previous interval is no longer present, suggesting that the additional deflection from \(30^\circ\) to \(34^\circ\) does not produce the same change in control-surface state.

The pitching-moment derivative shows larger discrepancies among the methods than the lift derivative.
The RANS simulations provide the closest overall agreement with the wind tunnel data, whereas the lifting-line results show larger deviations, consistent with the pitching-moment discrepancies discussed in the previous subsection.
At positive incidences, the magnitude of \(C_{M_y,\delta}\) decreases markedly when moving from the low-deflection interval \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) to the finite-deflection interval \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\).
This reduction indicates a loss of pitching-moment control effectiveness associated with the onset of control-surface separation and with the corresponding redistribution of the incremental aerodynamic load.
At negative incidences, the effectiveness changes less over the same deflection interval, indicating that the load distribution varies more nearly linearly with control-surface deflection when the deflected surface remains attached.

For the larger deflection intervals, the wind tunnel curves remain in a lower-effectiveness regime, and further increases in deflection do not produce a comparable additional reduction in \(C_{M_y,\delta}\).
In particular, for the interval \([20^\circ,30^\circ]\), the measured pitching-moment effectiveness becomes positive, approximately \(0.14\)--\(0.17\), for \(-8^\circ\leq\alpha\leq-2^\circ\), in the same range in which the lift effectiveness vanishes.
The additional deflection therefore produces a nose-up moment without additional lift, which indicates that load is lost near the trailing edge and partly recovered upstream of the aerodynamic center.
As the incidence becomes more negative, the control effectiveness increases again, suggesting that the local flow over the control surface returns to a more favorable state.
Once the deflected surface is substantially separated, at large deflections and large absolute incidences, the incremental pitching-moment response is governed primarily by load redistribution over the remaining attached and separated regions rather than by a simple increase in control-surface loading.

For the last interval, \(\delta\in[30^\circ,34^\circ]\), the wind tunnel pitching-moment effectiveness is low and affected by larger uncertainty.
As noted above, this interval is more sensitive to finite-difference noise because it is computed over a smaller deflection range.
Moreover, the numerical predictions deviate substantially from the experimental trend, particularly at negative incidence angles.
These conditions are challenging to simulate because the control surface operates near the transition between attached and separated flow.
As a result, small differences in the predicted separation onset produce large variations in the computed incremental lift and pitching-moment response.


Table~\ref{tab:control_effectiveness} summarizes the regular negative-incidence portions of Fig.~\ref{fig:AeroCoeffs:control_effectiveness}: \(\alpha\in[-6^\circ,0^\circ]\) for \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) and \(\alpha\in[-14^\circ,-4^\circ]\) for \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\), sampled every \(2^\circ\).
The intervals \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) and \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\) exhibit a sufficiently regular negative-incidence range and are therefore reduced to averaged reference values.
The larger deflection intervals, \([20^\circ,30^\circ]\) and \([30^\circ,34^\circ]\), are not included in the table because their derivative curves are strongly incidence-dependent and do not exhibit a clear reference plateau.
For these intervals, reducing the response to a single averaged value would obscure the separated-flow mechanisms already visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:AeroCoeffs:control_effectiveness}.
The \([30^\circ,34^\circ]\) interval is also more sensitive to finite-difference noise because it is computed over a narrower deflection range.

To compare the attached-branch response without fitting a constant derivative across separation, the reference effectiveness is defined here as the arithmetic mean over the stated incidence samples:
\begin{equation}
\overline{C}_{Q,\delta}^{[\delta_i,\delta_j]}
=
\frac{1}{N_{\mathcal{A}^{[\delta_i,\delta_j]}}}
\sum_{\alpha_k\in\mathcal{A}^{[\delta_i,\delta_j]}}
C_{Q,\delta}^{[\delta_i,\delta_j]}(\alpha_k).
\label{eq:control_effectiveness_average}
\end{equation}
The averaging range \(\mathcal{A}^{[\delta_i,\delta_j]}\) is selected from the wind tunnel derivative curve and then applied unchanged to the RANS and DUST lifting-line data.
This choice ensures that the comparison among fidelity levels is performed over the same incidence range. The resulting averages characterize those ranges only; the two intervals use different incidence samples and therefore do not isolate a pure deflection effect. All tabulated moments are evaluated about each method's own aerodynamic center. The wind tunnel uncertainty shown is the mean of the pointwise propagated uncertainty intervals, rather than a confidence interval of the mean.

The values reported in Table~\ref{tab:control_effectiveness} show that, on the negative-incidence branch, the control effectiveness decreases only moderately when the deflection interval is increased from \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) to \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\).
Across the two stated incidence ranges, the wind tunnel averaged lift effectiveness decreases from \(2.124\pm0.047\) to \(1.927\pm0.051\), a reduction of approximately \(9\%\).
The magnitude of pitching-moment effectiveness decreases by a similar fraction, from \(0.535\pm0.023\) to \(0.489\pm0.034\).
The ratio \(-\overline{C}_{M_y,\delta}/\overline{C}_{L,\delta}\) therefore remains close to \(0.25\) in both intervals.
This ratio defines an effective incremental moment arm, \(\ell_{\mathrm{eff}}/\bar{c}\equiv-\Delta M_y/(\bar{c}\,\Delta L)\). A positive value corresponds to a nose-down incremental moment accompanying positive incremental lift. It approximates a downstream load-centroid distance only if the incremental force is predominantly lift and contributions from drag, vertical offsets, and any residual couple are negligible. It is therefore used as a moment-to-lift diagnostic, not as a direct measurement of the load centroid. Its similar values in the two averaging ranges indicate a similar balance of incremental lift and moment.
The strong loss of effectiveness visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:AeroCoeffs:control_effectiveness} occurs instead in incidence: for \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\), the wind tunnel lift effectiveness falls from approximately \(1.9\) at \(\alpha=-4^\circ\) to \(1.0\) at \(\alpha=0^\circ\) and \(0.7\) at \(\alpha=2^\circ\), when the control surface separates.
A constant derivative is therefore adequate on the attached negative-incidence branch, but not across the onset of control-surface separation.

The RANS simulations overpredict the lift control effectiveness in both intervals, by approximately \(17\%\) for \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) and \(23\%\) for \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\).
The pitching-moment effectiveness is instead reproduced within approximately \(1\%\) in both intervals.
The RANS effective moment-arm ratio, approximately \(0.22\) and \(0.20\), is slightly lower than the experimental value.
The larger computed lift increment is therefore accompanied by a smaller nose-down moment per unit incremental lift.

The DUST lifting-line model overpredicts lift effectiveness by approximately \(19\%\) and \(22\%\), similar to RANS.
However, the magnitude of its pitching-moment effectiveness is overpredicted by approximately \(73\%\) for \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) and by approximately a factor of two for \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\).
Moreover, the lifting-line model predicts an increase in pitching-moment effectiveness between the two intervals, whereas the wind tunnel data show a decrease.
The effective moment-arm ratio is approximately \(0.37\) and \(0.42\) for the lifting-line model, compared with approximately \(0.25\) experimentally.
These ratios depend on the moment origin, so they do not alone establish an absolute load-centroid error.
The lifting-line aerodynamic center also lies approximately \(0.07~\mathrm{m}\) downstream of the experimental one (Table~\ref{tab:Delta0:AC}).
Transporting the numerical moments to the wind tunnel aerodynamic-center axis using the body-normal force increases the lifting-line moment-effectiveness discrepancy to factors of approximately \(2.2\) and \(2.5\), respectively.
Thus the moment discrepancy persists at a common reference axis and is larger than the lift discrepancy, indicating a limitation in the predicted load distribution.




\subsection{Main-surface stall and post-stall behavior}
\label{sec:main_stall_results}

The previous subsections focused on the nonlinearities associated with control-surface deflection and with the corresponding loss of control effectiveness.
These effects may occur even while the fixed portion of the tailplane remains largely attached.
A separate analysis is therefore required to distinguish control-surface-dominated separation from main-surface stall and from the subsequent post-stall behavior.

This subsection examines flow evolution at higher incidences, where separation extends upstream of the hinge line and modifies the global aerodynamic response of the complete tailplane.
The analysis is based on the RANS surface-flow topologies reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:FlowTopology:Delta_0_10_20_34}.
For each control-surface deflection, three representative states are selected from the RANS lift curve to describe comparable stages of stall evolution.

The first state corresponds to the incidence at which the RANS lift coefficient reaches its maximum value.
Under this condition, the fixed portion of the tailplane remains largely attached, and the flow state therefore represents the last high-lift condition predicted before the abrupt stall transition.
The second state corresponds to the first computed incidence after the maximum-lift condition.
This state documents the rapid development of main-surface separation and the associated loss of lift predicted by RANS.
The third state corresponds to a deep post-stall condition, selected after the main lift drop, when the separated-flow topology is fully developed.
The selected incidences therefore differ across deflections but correspond to comparable stages of the RANS stall and post-stall evolution.

The first column of Fig.~\ref{fig:FlowTopology:Delta_0_10_20_34} corresponds to the maximum-lift state predicted by RANS for each control-surface deflection.
For the lower deflections, the maximum lift coefficient is reached while the flow remains attached over most of the tailplane.
As deflection increases, a recirculating region first appears near the trailing edge and then progressively extends over the deflected control surface.
Because the main contribution to the integrated lift still comes from the fixed surface, the lift loss associated with control-surface separation does not prevent the development of the maximum lift coefficient.
At \(\delta=34^\circ\), the maximum-lift state occurs when separation already affects a substantial portion of the fixed surface.
Nevertheless, the inboard region remains largely attached.
Because this region has a larger local chord due to the tapered planform, it contributes significantly to the integrated lift.

The second column reports the first post-peak incidence, where the abrupt drop in \(C_L\) is visible in the RANS lift curve.
In the zero-deflection case, this condition is associated with a leading-edge separated region that extends over most of the span.
A strong suction peak remains visible within the separated structure, but the large recirculating region causes a sudden reduction in integrated lift.
At \(\delta=10^\circ\), the separated structure shifts downstream relative to the zero-deflection case and lies closer to the hinge region.
This downstream displacement becomes more evident for \(\DeltaSimple{20}\), where the recirculating core is mainly located over the control surface.
In both cases, the most inboard stations remain largely attached, consistent with the delayed separation observed near the root.
For the largest deflection, \(\DeltaSimple{34}\), the control surface is already separated over most of the span before the post-peak condition is reached.
The additional lift loss therefore comes from extending separation over the fixed surface rather than from the initial separation of the deflected surface.

The last column shows a deep-post-stall condition, selected near the minimum value of \(C_L\) predicted by RANS after the main lift drop.
For \(\delta=0^\circ\), the separated structure has moved further inboard, leaving only a narrow attached region near the root.
For the intermediate deflections, \(\DeltaSimple{10}\) and \(\DeltaSimple{20}\), the recirculating region associated with the control surface remains visible, while an additional separated region develops near the leading edge because of the larger incidence.
The resulting topology indicates that the post-stall state is governed by the interaction between the control-surface and main-surface separations.
For \(\DeltaSimple{34}\), the flow field becomes more irregular and does not exhibit a single well-defined recirculating cell.
This condition is particularly challenging for a steady RANS calculation because the control surface and the fixed surface are both largely separated, and small changes in the predicted separation pattern can produce substantial changes in the local load distribution.
The good agreement obtained for the integrated lift coefficient indicates that the dominant post-stall load level is reasonably captured.
However, because wind tunnel surface-flow visualization is not available at this incidence, interpret the detailed topology as a RANS-based explanation of the numerical result rather than a direct validation of the local separated-flow structure.

A direct one-to-one comparison between the wind tunnel visualizations and the RANS topologies is not possible because the two datasets reach stall through different paths.
The wind tunnel lift curves in Figure~\ref{fig:Coefficients:ExpvsNum:Delta0_10_20_34} show a gradual reduction of the \(C_L\)--\(\alpha\) slope and reach \(C_{L,\max}\) at higher incidences.
Surface-flow visualizations are available only at selected incidences.

The visualizations therefore correspond to the maximum-lift incidence for \(\delta=10^\circ\) and to incidences \(2^\circ\)--\(4^\circ\) beyond it for the other deflections.
Conversely, the RANS solutions remain largely attached to the fixed surface up to the predicted maximum-lift condition, then transition more abruptly to separated flow at the next incidence.

Figure~\ref{fig:FlowTopology:RANSVsExp} is therefore organized to separate the discrepancy in maximum-lift incidence from the discrepancy in separated-flow topology.
For each deflection, the first column reports the RANS topology at the RANS maximum-lift condition, the second column reports the wind-tunnel visualization closest to the experimental maximum-lift incidence, and the third column reports the RANS topology at the same incidence as the corresponding wind-tunnel visualization.

This layout allows comparison of the RANS maximum-lift state with the experimental topology near maximum lift, while also showing the numerical solution at the same incidence as the visualization.

A further limitation of the topology comparison concerns the treatment of the root region.
In the wind tunnel, the inboard part of the model is mounted on the floor and is affected by the floor boundary layer.
In the RANS simulations, the root is instead modeled using an ideal symmetry plane.
This difference can influence the local separation pattern near the root and the spanwise development of the separated structures.
Consequently, discrepancies in the inboard location, extent, or migration of the separated region should not be attributed only to turbulence or transition modeling.
This effect is expected to be most relevant in the stall and post-stall regimes, where the separated-flow topology involves a substantial portion of the span.

The numerical stall development is therefore more abrupt, with the separated topology appearing only after the maximum-lift point.
This difference explains the lower predicted stall incidence and the sharper drop in lift observed in the RANS coefficient curves.

When the RANS solution is evaluated at the same incidence as the wind tunnel visualization, the predicted flow topology becomes more similar to the experimental one.
In both datasets, the high-incidence state is characterized by a large separated region and by the formation of a coherent recirculating structure over the suction side.
However, the RANS-separated structure is generally located farther inboard than in the wind-tunnel visualization.
This spanwise displacement is consistent with the different treatment of the root boundary condition.
The wind-tunnel floor boundary layer can promote different inboard separation development, whereas the ideal symmetry plane used in the numerical model imposes a cleaner root condition and may alter the spanwise migration of the separated structure.

Overall, the comparison shows that RANS reproduces the main high-incidence separated-flow pattern observed in the wind tunnel, but predicts its development too abruptly and with the dominant separated structure displaced inboard.
The surface topologies therefore support the interpretation of the numerical stall mechanism and indicate that the experimental stall progression is more gradual and more affected by the inboard boundary condition.


\subsection{Regime-dependent model accuracy and multi-fidelity implications}
\label{sec:model_accuracy_results}

The previous analyses show that numerical-method accuracy depends strongly on the aerodynamic regime.
A single global statement on model performance would therefore be misleading, because the dominant physical mechanisms change from attached flow to control-surface separation, main-surface stall, and deep post-stall conditions.
This subsection summarizes the regime-dependent behavior of the DUST lifting-line and RANS predictions and indicates the regimes in which each method provides useful aerodynamic information.

The assessment is qualitative and based on the coefficient comparisons, control-effectiveness analysis, and surface-flow topologies discussed in the previous subsections.
No formal validity envelope is introduced because such an envelope would require application-dependent acceptance tolerances.
Instead, Table~\ref{tab:model_validity_summary} summarizes the practical implications of the observed discrepancies for each fidelity level.

In the attached flow regime, both numerical approaches reproduce the lift trend with reasonable accuracy.
The DUST lifting-line model is therefore useful for rapid estimates of the lift response and for preliminary exploration of the control-surface effect.
However, the pitching-moment prediction is less robust because it depends on both the sectional moment data and the spanwise distribution of the incremental load.

When control-surface separation develops, the limitations of the lifting-line approach become more evident.
The response is no longer governed by a simple sectional attached-flow increment, and load redistribution between the fixed surface and the control surface becomes important.
In this regime, RANS provides a more consistent physical description of the flow, although the predicted onset of separation may shift relative to the wind tunnel data.

In the main-stall and post-stall regimes, the lifting-line model should be regarded as outside its reliable range.
The RANS simulations capture the large-scale separated topology and the post-stall lift-retention mechanism associated with the positively swept planform.
However, the stall transition is predicted too abruptly, and the detailed separated-flow topology is sensitive to the root boundary condition and to the steady-flow assumption.
Consequently, RANS remains useful for qualitative interpretation and for estimating the post-stall load level, but its local topology should not be taken as a fully validated representation of the wind tunnel flow.











\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

A positively swept, zero-dihedral tailplane equipped with a full-span trailing-edge control surface was investigated using a multi-fidelity numerical--experimental approach.
Wind tunnel measurements, a nonlinear lifting-line model, and three-dimensional RANS simulations were compared over the full available incidence--deflection range, organized by the flow mechanism governing each regime.

The first nonlinearity introduced by deflection is the separation of the control surface, not the stall of the tailplane.
A positive deflection shifts the lift curve upward by increasing effective camber, but it also raises the adverse pressure gradient downstream of the hinge line.
The deflected surface therefore separates first, and the incidence at which this separation affects the lift curve decreases as the deflection increases.
Because the fixed surface carries most of the lift and remains largely attached, the global lift recovers and continues to grow after the control surface separates.
Control-surface separation thus appears as a local change in the lift and pitching-moment slopes well before maximum lift.
At \(\delta=34^\circ\), the control surface is separated over most of the incidence range.
Its contribution then dominates the drag in the range where the fixed surface is attached, so the drag curve becomes nearly linear rather than parabolic and the minimum drag increases.

Control-surface separation also reduces control effectiveness.
While the deflected surface remains attached, the response to deflection is nearly linear: on the negative-incidence branch, the measured lift and pitching-moment effectiveness decrease by approximately \(9\%\) between the \([0^\circ,10^\circ]\) and \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\) intervals.
The corresponding effective moment-to-lift arm remains approximately \(0.25\) mean aerodynamic chords in the two selected averaging ranges; this is not a direct load-centroid measurement.
The strong loss of effectiveness occurs in incidence instead: for \([10^\circ,20^\circ]\), the measured lift effectiveness falls by approximately a factor of two between \(\alpha=-4^\circ\) and \(\alpha=0^\circ\), when the control surface separates.
RANS reproduces the pitching-moment effectiveness within approximately \(1\%\) on the attached branch, but it overpredicts the lift effectiveness by \(17\)--\(23\%\) and delays the onset of control-surface separation.
The lifting-line model gives a similar lift effectiveness but overpredicts the pitching-moment effectiveness by \(73\%\) to a factor of two about its own aerodynamic center. The discrepancy becomes larger at a common moment axis, indicating an error in the predicted load distribution beyond the lift-magnitude error.

The tailplane reaches maximum lift through different paths in the experiment and in the simulations.
In the wind tunnel, separation develops progressively, the lift-curve slope decreases gradually, and the surface-flow visualizations show extensive separated flow at or shortly after the maximum-lift incidence.
In RANS, the fixed surface remains largely attached up to the predicted maximum lift, and separation then spreads over the fixed surface within one incidence step; without deflection, it forms a leading-edge separated region over most of the span.
After stall, both datasets retain significant lift, consistent with the coherent vortical structure that forms over the suction side of the swept surface.
At the visualization incidences, RANS reproduces the main separated-flow pattern, but the dominant separated structure is located farther inboard.
This displacement is consistent with the ideal symmetry plane used at the root in the simulations, in place of the floor boundary layer that is present in the experiment.

These mechanisms define the useful range of each fidelity level for this configuration.
The lifting-line model is adequate for lift trends in attached and weakly nonlinear conditions, but not for pitching moment, control effectiveness, or post-stall behavior.
RANS extends the useful range to control-surface separation and gives a physically consistent description of the separated-flow topology, but it predicts an excessively abrupt stall transition and a root-sensitive post-stall topology.

Future work should focus on the flow regimes in which the present comparison identified the largest model-form sensitivity.
Additional RANS simulations reproducing the wind tunnel floor and root-region boundary layer would help isolate the effect of the experimental mounting condition on the spanwise development of the separated structures.
This point is particularly relevant near \(C_{L,\max}\) and in deep-stall conditions, where the comparison with the available surface-flow visualizations indicates that the root boundary condition can influence the inboard position of the dominant separated region.
Scale-resolving simulations, such as delayed detached-eddy simulations, should also be considered at selected maximum-lift and deep-stall conditions to assess whether the sharp stall transition predicted by steady RANS results from the steady-flow assumption.
Finally, a more detailed investigation of control-surface separation is required to characterize the onset, spanwise growth, and possible hysteresis of the separated region over the deflected surface.
Such analyses would provide a more complete basis for improving reduced-order control-effectiveness models and for defining the limits of applicability of nonlinear lifting-line methods in the presence of full-span control-surface deflection.
